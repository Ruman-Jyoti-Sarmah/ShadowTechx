Notes from the studio

Things we've learned the hard way.

Three notes on scoping, technology and handover. They're the same things we say to clients on the first call — written down so we don't have to repeat ourselves.

Planning · 5 min read

How to scope a first release without lying to yourself

The first version of a product is not a smaller copy of the final one. It's the shortest route that lets one real person finish one real task — and tells you whether the idea holds up.

Start by writing down who the user is and what they're doing today instead. Not "manage inventory" — "Rekha writes tomorrow's stock list in a notebook every evening." That sentence is worth more than a page of features.

Then cut. Walk the path from first click to finished task. Anything that doesn't survive on that path goes into a second list. Not deleted, just parked.

  • One primary user, one primary task.
  • Something you can measure: is the task actually easier now?
  • List the outside dependencies early — payments, SMS, government APIs.
  • Keep the parked list visible. It's your roadmap.

A short scope isn't a lack of ambition. It's the only way to find out if the thing works before you've spent nine months on it.

Engineering · 4 min read

Pick the stack after you know the constraints

Framework comparisons are everywhere. What's rare is knowing which constraints actually matter for your project. Where does it have to run? What does it have to talk to? Who maintains it in two years?

Those questions usually cut the list down on their own. If it needs to work offline on a shop counter, that's a desktop app. If it needs to reach both app stores on a small budget, it's one shared codebase. If it's a page that should load in under a second, it's probably not a framework at all.

Pick something that solves today's problem clearly and has somewhere to go if it grows. Don't add infrastructure for a load you don't have yet, and don't pick a tool because a blog post said it was the future.

Most importantly: write down why you chose it. In a year the trade-off will be obvious to nobody.

Delivery · 4 min read

Sort out the handover before launch week

Launch week is the worst possible time to discover nobody knows whose name the domain is in. Work out the boring parts while the project is still being scoped, not the day before it goes live.

Decide early: who owns the GitHub repo, who pays for hosting, where the environment variables live, and who can deploy a fix at 11pm. Secrets belong in a password manager or a secrets store — never pasted into a file in the repository.

Then actually do it once together. Deploy a small change, break something on purpose, fix it. Reading instructions is not the same as having done it.

When we hand over, everything is already in the client's name and we walk through it together. It takes an hour and saves months of confused emails.

These are our own notes from real projects. They're not legal, security or technical advice for your specific situation — ask us about yours.
